# Insurance_Pricing_Project
The project was designed as an end-to-end insurance pricing and portfolio optimization study, combining predictive modelling, actuarial risk assessment, customer behaviour analysis and mathematical optimization. 

In Part 1, I first conducted a comprehensive analysis of the insurance quotation data and prepared the dataset for modelling. The main business objective was to estimate the probability that a customer would accept an insurance quotation, using the observed Quotation Acceptance Status as the target variable. After data cleaning, preprocessing, feature engineering and the treatment of categorical and numerical variables, I developed several classification models, including Logistic Regression, Random Forest, XGBoost, LightGBM and CatBoost. The models were trained and tuned using cross-validation and hyperparameter optimization, and their performances were compared using discrimination, classification and probability-quality metrics such as ROC-AUC, Precision, Recall, F1-score, KS, Brier Score, calibration, lift and cumulative gain. 

Logistic Regression was used as an interpretable baseline, while the tree-based ensemble models were introduced to capture potentially nonlinear relationships between customer, vehicle, quotation and pricing characteristics. The model comparison showed that the tree-based approaches provided stronger predictive performance, with XGBoost achieving the highest ROC-AUC while LightGBM providing a highly competitive overall performance and stronger operational characteristics in terms of metrics such as KS and F1. Importantly, the objective was not simply to classify customers as accepters or non-accepters, but to obtain reliable acceptance probabilities that could subsequently be used as inputs to an insurance pricing decision process. 

In Part 2, I extended this predictive framework into an actuarial pricing and portfolio optimization framework. Since actual claim frequency and claim severity information was not available, I constructed a Claim Risk Proxy using the available policyholder and vehicle characteristics and explicitly treated this measure as a risk-cost proxy rather than a true Expected Loss estimate. Customers were subsequently differentiated into Low, Medium and High Risk segments, allowing the relationship between premium, risk exposure and quotation acceptance to be evaluated from an actuarial perspective. I then introduced a price-response framework in which discounts between 0% and 10%, in 0.1 percentage-point increments, were simulated for customers and the corresponding changes in acceptance probability, expected accepted policies, premium revenue, discount cost, uplift and price elasticity were estimated. This allowed me to quantify how sensitive individual customers were to changes in premium and to identify situations in which a relatively small premium concession could generate a meaningful increase in the probability of acceptance. At the portfolio level, I evaluated the trade-off between discount expenditure and expected portfolio growth and constructed a Pareto frontier to identify economically efficient discount scenarios. A knee point was then selected as a practical balance between additional discount cost and incremental portfolio growth, and the associated discount expenditure was used as the available optimization budget. Instead of applying the same discount to every customer, I formulated a customer-level Mixed Integer Linear Programming optimization problem in which the available discount budget was allocated across individual customers to maximize expected portfolio growth while respecting the 0–10% discount constraint and ensuring one pricing decision per customer. The optimization therefore considered the economic value of each potential discount rather than treating all customers equally. For every customer, the final framework can provide an optimal discount, resulting premium, baseline and optimized acceptance probabilities, expected uplift, price elasticity, risk score, risk segment, expected discount cost and discount efficiency. 

I also conducted robustness and constraint validation tests covering solution feasibility, discount bounds, budget compliance, Pareto consistency, price-response monotonicity, probability stress testing and discount-budget sensitivity. From an actuarial and insurance management perspective, the overall framework therefore moves beyond traditional acceptance prediction and establishes a decision-support system that connects customer behaviour with risk and pricing economics. The central business recommendation is that the insurer should not pursue portfolio growth through a blanket discount strategy; instead, discounts should be selectively allocated to customers where the expected increase in acceptance justifies the associated premium sacrifice and risk exposure. In this way, machine learning provides the behavioural probability estimates, actuarial analysis provides the risk perspective, price elasticity quantifies customer sensitivity, and optimization converts these estimates into an economically efficient portfolio-level pricing strategy.
